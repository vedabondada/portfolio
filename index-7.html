Discovery

The insight didn't come from a roadmap — it came from listening. While conducting user research for an entirely different feature, a customer mentioned confusion about what was in versus out of their project proposal. That one comment opened a much bigger question: why did Oracle's workflow only begin after a project was approved? The pre-project phase had no tooling at all. I saw a zero-to-one opportunity in a phase of the lifecycle the product had never touched.

"The gap wasn't in the product — it was in the lifecycle. Oracle supported projects after kickoff. Nobody owned what came before."
Pitch & approval

To make the idea tangible, I partnered with an intern to build a working Figma prototype — something a VP could click through, not just read about. The value proposition was dual-layered: this was both an AI use case and an entirely new product segment. Not just a feature — Oracle entering a phase of the project lifecycle it had never addressed. The pitch landed immediately. We got approval and a dedicated cross-functional team within the same conversation.

"A clickable prototype does more in five minutes than a slide deck does in fifty."
Customer research

Before writing a single prompt, we went back to customers. We asked enterprise clients to share real past proposals — building a corpus from the field, not assumptions. Through structured interviews we mapped what was universal versus exceptional. What emerged was clear: four sections appear in every professional services proposal — executive summary, company background, implementation plan, and resource plan. That became the foundation of everything we built.

"We didn't design the proposal structure — our customers already had one. We just made it visible."
UX design

The design principle was simple: it should feel like filling out a form, not using AI. We built a 3-step flow — input context, generate, review and publish. Users spend 1-2 minutes filling in client name, industry, project description, and dates. Hit generate. In 20 seconds they have a full editable proposal across all four sections, each independently regenerable. The review step is read-only — a deliberate pause that builds trust before the proposal leaves the building.

"The best AI features don't feel like AI. They just feel fast."

Prompt engineering

Four sections meant four distinct prompts — each tuned for different tone, temperature, and scope. The executive summary: concise and confident. Company background: relevant to this specific proposal. Implementation and resource plans: granular enough that a client could evaluate the approach. Getting the guardrails right was as important as the prompts themselves — each prompt had to know explicitly what not to say, because the other three sections would cover it. We worked with Oracle's AI Prompt Lab on RAG pipeline design, using Cohere Command R+ for its native grounded generation.

"A prompt that doesn't know its boundaries will step on every other prompt in the system."
Testing & iteration

Three-tier testing pipeline: each prompt tested individually first — data accuracy before tone. Then human evaluation: internal team, extended Oracle PPM org, enterprise customers. 30+ iterations per prompt before beta. The most important insight came when we tested all four sections together — they were repeating each other. We added cross-section guardrails. Beta ran with 8 enterprise clients tracking edit rates. By launch, edits dropped from 3-4 per section to 1-2.

"Individual prompt quality doesn't guarantee system quality. The real test is when all four run together."
Launch & go-to-market

Shipping wasn't the end — adoption was the goal. I authored the full release readiness module: UX guide, business benefit framing, enablement steps, and an AI trust explainer. I recorded a product demo for self-serve rollout. And for enterprise clients who were skeptical, I ran 8 individual 45-minute workshops — not to sell the feature, but to help them understand how to trust it. That direct customer engagement drove the adoption number we hit.

"Adoption doesn't happen at launch. It happens in the room with the skeptic."
Impact

90% adoption across all Oracle PPM customers. Proposal creation time went from 3-4 days to 10-15 minutes — with AI generation completing in 20 seconds. Edit rates dropped roughly 50% from beta to launch. 8 enterprise clients in beta. And beyond the metrics: Oracle now has a product presence in a phase of the project lifecycle it had never addressed before.

"The 90% number matters. But the bigger outcome is that Oracle now plays in a segment it didn't before."

Discovery & prioritization

As Oracle PPM shifted from generative AI features to agents that take action, the team ran a prioritization exercise across the entire project lifecycle. We mapped every manual, time-consuming workflow and scored them on three dimensions: time cost, frequency, and business criticality. From a longlist of 12 potential agents, 4 were identified as highest-value — all sitting in the order-to-cash lifecycle, where deals become billable projects. I was assigned these 4.

"The most painful workflows aren't always the most obvious ones. They're the ones people have learned to live with."
Customer validation

Before writing a single requirement, I went to customers. I sat with project setup analysts, project accountants, and PMO teams to understand how they actually worked through a deal package. What I found changed the product direction: nobody thought of OD, PO, and contract review as three separate jobs. They were one continuous workflow. The same person opened all three documents, cross-referenced them, and validated the whole package together. Three separate agents would force users to context-switch for what felt like one task.

"Users don't think in system boundaries. They think in tasks. Build for the task."
Consolidation decision

Leadership had already socialized a roadmap with 4 distinct agents. I proposed consolidating the first 3 into a single Contract Validation Assistant — and it created friction. The concerns were real: scope change, timeline risk, re-explaining the roadmap to stakeholders. The case I made was grounded in customer research. Users don't launch three separate agents — they think: "validate my project setup package." One assistant, one workflow, one place to review exceptions. The consolidation was approved.

"Pushing back on a committed roadmap is uncomfortable. Shipping the wrong product is worse."
Requirements & PRDs

I wrote full PRDs for both agents. For the validation assistant: document type classification, field extraction logic, business rule validation, exception reporting format, and human review workflow. For billing events creation: source data mapping from Order Management and CPQ, milestone identification, event structure, and the human approval step. Every agent had a non-negotiable human review gate — no autonomous writes to the ERP without explicit approval.

"In enterprise ERP, an agent that writes without human approval isn't a feature. It's a liability."

How the agents work

The Contract Validation Assistant is triggered from within Oracle Fusion when a deal package arrives. It retrieves attached documents from the system, classifies each document type, extracts relevant fields, validates them against PPM setup requirements, then produces a structured exception report. The human reviews exceptions only — not the full document. The Billing Events Creation Agent reads upstream data from Order Management and CPQ, identifies contract milestones, drafts billing events with names, dates, amounts, and triggers, and presents them for human review before anything is written to the project contract.

"The shift from full document review to exception review is where the time savings actually live."
Testing

I built the test plan covering validation accuracy, edge cases, and exception handling. Testing ran in phases: internal team, then extended PPM org, then controlled rollout with enterprise customers. Key scenarios included missing fields, funding mismatches, date inconsistencies, and multi-reviewer contracts. Validation accuracy targets were met before general availability — 70-90% of issues surfaced before project setup, compared to post-setup discovery in the manual process.

"Catching a funding mismatch before project setup takes minutes. Catching it after takes weeks of rework."
Launch & go-to-market

I authored release readiness material for both agents — covering what each agent does, how to enable it, how to interpret exception reports, and how to trust AI-generated billing events before approving them. The trust piece was critical. These agents touch billing and revenue recognition — areas where customers are naturally skeptical of automation. The documentation addressed that skepticism directly, explaining the human review gate and what the agents can and cannot do autonomously.

"When AI touches revenue, trust isn't assumed. It's earned through transparency."
Impact

70-90% reduction in billing event creation effort. Document review time reduced 50-80%. 70-90% of funding and PO mismatches now caught before project setup. Project setup cycle time improved 20-40%. Configuration errors reduced 20-50%. The lifecycle story: deals move from signed contract to active billable project faster, with fewer mistakes and less rework across project setup, finance, and billing teams.

"The metric that mattered most to leadership: faster time from signed contract to first invoice."

Grant Summit

Grants Management was one of the first products I owned at Oracle. At the annual Grant Summit — where customers come to learn about what's new in the platform — I saw an opportunity. Instead of just presenting to users, I proposed we turn part of the summit into a research session. We told customers: we want to build what you actually need. The response was overwhelming. Dozens of users signed up to participate in the research program that followed.

"The best product research happens when users believe you're actually listening."
Survey research

We sent structured surveys to the participants — grants administrators, principal investigators, and grants accountants. The survey covered time spent on key tasks, pain points with the current platform, workflow bottlenecks, and usability frustrations. 45 users responded, giving us a quantitative baseline of where the platform was falling short. That data shaped which problems we prioritized for deeper investigation.

"Surveys tell you what hurts. Interviews tell you why."
User interviews

From the survey pool, I selected participants who surfaced the most interesting pain points. We ran 20-25 one-hour interviews — structured enough to be comparable, open enough to surface things we hadn't thought to ask about. What came back was a detailed picture of how grants administrators actually moved through their work, where they got stuck, what workarounds they had built, and what would genuinely make their day easier.

"Users will tell you about workarounds they've built as if they're normal. That's where the product opportunities live."
Prioritization

With all the insights documented, I built a prioritized backlog. The framework was simple: start with low-hanging fruit that removes friction fast, then move to UX improvements, then tackle deeper workflow and integration features. No feature made it onto the roadmap without a direct link back to user feedback. If we couldn't point to the user insight that justified it, it didn't get built.

"Prioritization is easy when every item on your list has a user behind it."

What we built

The 12 features spanned three tiers of complexity. Quick wins first: simple UI fixes like surfacing an edit button buried in a dropdown. Then UX improvements: dashboards, better data filtering, improved grant status visibility. Then deeper functionality: improvements to FBDI file-based data import and workflow enhancements. The sequencing was intentional — early wins built trust with users that we were actually listening.

"Ship the easy wins first. It earns you the credibility to tackle the hard ones."
Execution

Over 18 months, we shipped consistently — feature by feature, quarter by quarter. Each feature went through the full product cycle: requirements, design, engineering, QA, release readiness. The research was the foundation, but execution required staying close to the same user community throughout — checking back, validating direction, and making sure what we were building matched what they had described.

"A research project isn't done when the interviews end. It's done when the last feature ships."
Validation

The next year's Grant Summit became the validation moment. We went back to the same community and asked them to rate the platform. The feedback was direct: the product felt significantly more intuitive, the friction points they had raised were gone, and new users were getting up to speed much faster. The satisfaction score came in at 4.7 out of 5.

"Going back to the same users who gave you the research is the most honest form of validation."
Impact

4.7 out of 5 user satisfaction at the following Grant Summit. 40% reduction in onboarding learning curve. 12 features shipped across 18 months, all traceable back to specific user insights. 45 survey participants, 20-25 in-depth interviews. The less quantifiable outcome: a user community that trusted Oracle PPM enough to keep showing up and keep giving feedback.

"The best metric isn't satisfaction score. It's whether users come back next year to give you more feedback."

The spark

In my first year at Oracle, I noticed something early: the product was genuinely powerful, but the experience felt like it belonged to a different decade. Workflows took too many clicks, navigation was unintuitive, and newer users struggled to ramp up. I had some bandwidth and a summer intern who was equally bothered by it. We picked one workflow and redesigned it from 8 clicks to 4. No formal team, no budget, no roadmap item. Just a Figma prototype and a conviction that the platform could feel much better.

"The best initiatives don't wait for permission. They show up with a prototype."
Pitching leadership

Once the prototype was compelling enough, I organized a presentation with product leaders across Oracle Fusion PPM. The pitch was simple: the platform's capabilities are strong, but the experience is holding adoption back — especially for new users. We got approval for a formal redesign initiative. A cross-functional team was formed: two additional PMs, three UX designers, and a UX researcher. What started as a side project with an intern became an officially resourced program.

"A well-timed prototype is the most persuasive document in product."
Three-team approach

Everyone on the team had strong opinions — which is exactly the problem with internal-opinion-driven design. Instead of debating which direction to pursue, I proposed splitting into three separate design teams, each exploring a different philosophy. We made it a friendly internal competition. Once all three designs were ready, we ran structured user research sessions — 45-60 minutes each — where real users walked through all three experiences. Then we combined the strongest elements into one final experience. Users made the decision, not us.

"Let internal opinions diverge early. Let user research converge late."
Outcome

The redesign framework became the foundation for Oracle's next-generation UX direction within Project Portfolio Management. The full external rollout was later absorbed into a broader initiative as Oracle shifted toward generative AI and agentic workflows — but the design system, research foundation, and workflow principles we established continue to influence the product's direction today.

"The work didn't ship as a standalone release. It shipped as the DNA of everything that came after."
